Where the money actually goes

Auction calculators assume a generic split. Yours doesn't follow one — running backs alone eat almost half the room's cash, and defense is treated as an afterthought worth less than a rounding error.

RB 45.4% WR 38.7% QB 7.6% TE 7.4% DEF 0.9%

The room also spends almost exactly to the dollar every year — league-average spend has been $195–$198 of the $200 budget in all six seasons. Nobody sits on cash; if a team looks capped out late, they almost certainly are.

Your scoring rules make the RB obsession rational, not just habit: this is 0.5 PPR (half point per reception, not a full point), no kicker roster spot at all, and a standard 4-point passing TD / 6-point rushing & receiving TD split. Half-PPR quietly favors touch-and-score volume over target volume — exactly RB territory — over the reception-dependent WR/slot game a full-PPR league would reward more. The market is pricing your actual rules correctly.

What actually wins here

Six champions, six completely different draft-day allocations. RB share among title winners ranges from 12% (Malvyn, 2022 — a receiver-heavy build) to 66% (William, 2020 — almost the opposite). Across all 71 team-seasons on record, no position-spend metric correlates strongly with wins or points scored — the strongest relationship found (quarterback spend share vs. points scored) is still weak (r = 0.23). There is no winning formula by budget allocation alone in this league's history. What wins is picking the right players inside whatever shape you choose — which is exactly why the value-zone and per-position sections below matter more than a "spend X% here" rule would.

Your own results back this up in miniature: your single best season, 2022 (10–4, 1,678 PF — your highest ever by nearly 200 points), was also your most concentrated RB-heavy draft on record (85% RB share, one pick eating 73% of your position spend). But your two worst finishes (2020, 11th; 2021, 12th) were also RB-heavy builds (68% and 64% RB share). Same general shape, opposite outcomes — the allocation alone doesn't explain either result. This data can't separate player evaluation from plain injury luck (a stars-and-scrubs build is especially exposed if your one or two RB studs get hurt), and 2020/2021 may well have been hit by both. Read it as "allocation isn't the lever," not "your picks were bad those years."

Value zones: where to spend, where to wait

Your price curve isn't smooth — it has real cliffs (a rank where price drops sharply, meaning the player right after the cliff is comparatively cheap for similar tier) and real plateaus (a stretch where price barely moves, meaning you can be patient without losing much). Named against your actual 2026 board:

Where to spend

  • True TE1, if you're going to pay at all. The TE1→TE2 gap is the single steepest cliff on the entire board (−36%, Trey McBride $43 → Brock Bowers $28). Historically Malvyn wins this fight; if you want in on it, know it's expensive by design, not by accident.
  • RB13–15 the moment you see a name you like. This tier erodes fast (−11 to −12% per rank: Breece Hall → Chase Brown → Bucky Irving) — waiting one more nomination here costs real money, unlike the plateaus below.

Where to wait — the plateaus

  • RB5–RB9 (James Cook III, Ashton Jeanty, De'Von Achane, Saquon Barkley, Derrick Henry): only ~5% average drop per rank. Your specific preference inside this tier costs almost nothing extra to indulge.
  • RB16–RB20 (Kenneth Walker III through Chuba Hubbard): flattens again after the RB13–15 crash. No rush once you're past pick 15 at the position.
  • WR11 and beyond (Ladd McConkey, Brian Thomas Jr., Marvin Harrison Jr., …): the room pays a real premium for WR10 (Drake London, $29) over WR11 ($28 — only $1 less on paper, but the jump into WR10 itself costs 13.5% more than WR11 costs over WR12). The discount starts right at #11 and stays gentle for ten more ranks — this is the deepest, safest plateau on the board.
  • QB8 and beyond (Bo Nix and later): QB cratered 27% from #1 to #2 alone and keeps falling hard through #9. Reinforces what the room already does — real average QB spend is $15 for two players. Don't pay QB2–5 prices; stream from the $8-and-under tier instead.
  • TE6 and beyond (Sam LaPorta and later): if you're not winning the TE1 fight, skip the TE2–5 tier entirely — $14–$28 for a TE that isn't actually elite is the single worst value trap on the board given the 36% cliff already spent at the top.

Keeper stash candidates for 2027

The rule is a hard cutoff, not a range: a player can only ever be kept if this year's draft price is $10 or less. Draft them at $11+ and they are not keeper-eligible next year at any price, no matter how well they play. If a pick is eligible, next year's keeper price is this year's price + $10 (so a $10 pick becomes a $20 keeper — the ceiling). Your 2026 game plan

  1. There's no formula to copy — commit to picking players well, not a position split. Champions have run everything from 12% to 66% RB share. Your own best (2022) and worst (2020, 2021) seasons were all RB-heavy builds — the shape didn't decide it, the players inside it did. Don't over-plan the allocation; spend that energy on the value zones instead.
  2. Bank Quinshon Judkins' $15 keeper as an RB slot filled, then budget ~$185 for the rest. He's off the board before the auction starts. Treat your real spending power as $185, not $200, when you're setting max bids in the live tool.
  3. Let Malvyn win the top tight ends and mid-tier QBs. He overpays there more than anyone else in the room (TE 2.45x, QB 1.94x league-average share). Competing head-on just inflates the price for both of you — reallocate instead. Watch whether Kassandra or Kerri (both DEF/TE/QB-heavy by their own history) join that fight too.
  4. Don't pay early for a defense. DEF is 0.9% of all league spend ever, and Scott (0.78x league norm) actively punts it. It will fall to the $1 bin regardless of what you do — nominate it late and let it happen.

  6. Budget for keepers up to $19–20, not $10. The rule is draft price + $10 for one keeper year, capped at a $10-or-under original price (so $20 is the hard ceiling) — the league's actual range has been $1–$19. A real chunk of the room's money is already spoken for before the auction opens.
